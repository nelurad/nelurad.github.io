What you are watching

  1. Overproduction

    In the first years of life, neurons form synapses much faster than they will need them. Synapse density in human cortex peaks in early childhood, well above adult levels.

  2. Use strengthens

    When one neuron repeatedly helps the next one fire, the synapse between them gets stronger. Here, each experience drives one pathway again and again.

  3. Weak synapses get tagged

    Synapses that rarely carry signals weaken. Complement proteins (C1q and C3) mark them for removal. They turn dashed coral here, and can still be rescued if they get used.

  4. Microglia remove them

    Microglia, the brain's resident immune cells, find tagged synapses and engulf them. The green cells roam the tissue and do exactly that.

Try this

Turn off Music before age 5 and watch its gold pathway fade, get tagged, and get eaten. Turn it back on later and the pathway stays gone.

In this model, removed synapses never regrow. Real brains still form new synapses throughout life, just far fewer, and some of the window for easy rewiring closes in childhood.

How this relates to your Python network

In neural_network.py, the weights W1 and W2 play the role of synapse strengths. That network learns by backpropagation, which uses calculus to adjust every weight at once. This model uses a local rule instead: a synapse strengthens when the neuron before it fires just before the neuron after it.

Machine learning also prunes. After training, deleting the smallest weights often costs little accuracy.

This model is a simplified picture. A human cortex has around 100 trillion synapses, and different regions prune on different schedules. Visual areas peak within the first year, while prefrontal cortex keeps pruning into the twenties.
